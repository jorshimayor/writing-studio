## The short version

1. **Raise the floor.** Make your weakest videos better before trying to make a big one bigger.
2. **Package before you record.** The thumbnail gives a reason to stop scrolling; the title gives a reason to click.
3. **Aim titles at "the stretch":** a more intriguing framing of what the video genuinely delivers.
4. **Script the first 15 to 20 seconds.** Re-confirm the click, then go straight into content.
5. **Guard the first six minutes.** Re-engage the viewer around 1, 3 and 6 minutes.
6. **The front half grows the channel. The back half builds fans.**
7. **End inside the last point, then cut.** No separate outro speech.
8. **One long-form video a week, over 8 minutes,** is the starting cadence. Consistency beats volume.
9. **Shorts, X and Discord feed the main channel.**
10. **Judge a strategy after 4 to 6 months,** not after 4 videos.

These defaults are for growth videos aimed at new viewers. Deep dives for existing followers can take a different shape (see [when to break the formula](#when-to-break-the-formula)).

## How the algorithm behaves

- YouTube optimises for keeping people on YouTube. Videos that hold attention get pushed, whatever the reason people keep watching.
- It matches **watch histories**, not categories or tags. Your video sits in a set of viewers' histories and gets recommended to people whose histories overlap. Recommendations draw on roughly the last few hundred videos someone watched.
- How it measures satisfaction isn't public. The working assumption: did people watch through, and if they left, did they come back?
- **Early bounces hurt.** If the wrong viewer clicks and leaves fast, YouTube reads that as a broken promise and stops serving the video. Packaging should also make the wrong person keep scrolling.
- **Average view duration (AVD) matters more than click-through rate (CTR).** CTR tells you whether the packaging works. AVD drives distribution.
- **Subscriber counts matter less than average views.** Subscribers follow views, so improve views first.
- A weak or off-topic upload can change who sees, and how hard YouTube tests, the next one.

## Strategy: pick a position

### Raise the floor first
- Your goal is a better worst video, not one viral hit. Once your typical video reliably reaches a solid baseline, you can hold a steadier cadence.
- Avoid big bets that can sink a month: a long, expensive production can underperform a simple, well-packaged video.
- You're trading for developer attention. Earn it as efficiently as you can while still giving real value.
- Small retention gains compound across 20 videos into a higher baseline.

### Then choose a lane

| Position | You get | You give up |
|---|---|---|
| Broad, no ceiling | Big spikes and reach beyond the niche | A steady floor |
| Specific niche | Reliable views and advertiser fit | Hypergrowth |
| Passion project | Videos that can break out | Predictability and return on effort |

- Most channels mix these. Aim for mostly videos you believe in, plus the occasional obvious format video.
- Create for the audience you want. A viral video in the wrong lane can lock in the wrong audience.
- Carve out your own place instead of copying a creator.
- Good education **creates the need, then satisfies it**: plant a problem only your video answers.
- Keep your voice. A calm teacher who never makes people feel stupid stands out, and personality currently beats pure tutorials in developer education.
- Weigh every growth tactic against whether you'll still want to film in six months.

### Commit
Run a strategy for **4 to 6 months**, tune it rather than replacing it, and expect the start to feel rough while your process and editor settle in. If results land far off target, make YouTube a supporting channel.

## Packaging: title, thumbnail, description

**The job:** stop the scroll, earn the click, make the watch enjoyable. Decide the title and thumbnail **before** recording. The package sets the promise, and the promise shapes what you film.

The thumbnail and title do different jobs. Don't repeat one in the other. Within about 30 seconds, viewers forget the thumbnail, so it only has to make them feel something for a moment. Package growth videos for people who don't know you yet: the video can be intimate, the packaging has to create broad curiosity.

### Titles

Think in **reasons to click**, not descriptions of the content.

| Level | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Truth** | States exactly what the video is. Few click, because they don't know they have the problem |
| **Stretch** | One step past the literal truth and still fully deliverable. The viewer asks "why?" or "how?" **Aim here** |
| **Clickbait** | Promises what the video can't pay off. Gets clicks, then trains viewers to distrust you |

How to find the stretch: write the smallest literal version, then ask what sits just outside it. The test: it should make someone think "huh?", not "I don't believe that."

- Sell the idea behind the product, not the product name. Names outsiders don't know limit reach.
- Use the term people already use over a more precise one that's too new.
- Don't promise a payoff that only arrives at the end. Most viewers won't get there.
- Use **Title Case** (it matches the profile and reads as cared-for).
- Before filming, search the title idea on YouTube and watch the top results: what worked, what to avoid, which angles they missed.
- Brainstorm titles in batches with a peer or your editor. The winner is often two weaker ideas combined.

### Thumbnails

- **Layout:** the idea, plus a recognisable enemy or saviour as the secondary subject, plus your face, in one or two consistent positions.
- **Aim for no text.** If you use text, one or two big words as decoration, not as the reason to click. Enlarge any UI text so it reads on a phone.
- **Evocative beats specific:** the thing that makes everyone feel the pain lands harder than the precise tool.
- **Use the first obvious metaphor.** If you thought of it first, so will the audience.
- **Magnify the action** in technical visuals: the one button or line, not the whole screen.
- Memes perform unevenly, because someone always hasn't seen the meme.
- **Squint test:** if the point still reads when you squint, it works.
- AI-generated art is acceptable only if it looks specific and human-made.
- Match the facial expression to the promise, and keep a library of portrait expressions so you don't need a custom shoot each time.
- Watch the channel page as a whole. Too many thumbnails in one style looks low-effort. If two options test about the same, pick the one that adds variety.
- Design follows the profile's brand kit (in this profile, the designer owns it). The skill provides the **concept and any text**, not the design.

### Descriptions
- One or two plain, search-friendly sentences, then links (article, repo, newsletter). Follow-along links go lower.
- **Chapter timestamps:** leave them off growth videos, because desktop viewers hover, preview the structure and leave before you've persuaded them. Add them to reference-style tutorials where viewers genuinely need to jump to a step.
- Tags play little part in distribution. Add a few accurate ones if you like, and don't spend time on them.

### Formats
- When a package works, turn it into a **repeatable format**, so not every video is a new creative battle. A format is worth repeating if follow-ups keep most of the original's views.
- Space formats out (roughly monthly) so the channel doesn't go stale, and rotate topics so you don't become "the one-product channel".
- Use experiments to discover formats, then ask what the repeatable version is.

## Plan and script

### Write the video in four layers

| Layer | What to write |
|---|---|
| Packaging | The title and thumbnail idea |
| Intro | 2 to 3 sentences, 15 to 20 seconds, word for word |
| Framework | 2 to 3 sentences on how the video delivers the promise ("I'll deploy a token on a testnet to show where most tutorials leak keys") |
| Macro points | The major beats inside the framework |

Skip micro-content (jokes, flourishes) at this stage. If something adds a little but costs a lot of effort, drop it.

- **Script the intro word for word.** Bullet-point the body.
- Check whether the bullets feel like a video. **One or two points means it's a Short or an X post,** not a long-form video.
- Write several intros and read them aloud. Bloat shows when spoken.

### The intro

Its only job is to **re-confirm the click**, because viewers forget why they clicked almost immediately.

- Shape: what changed, why you should care, what you'll get, then the first useful point.
- Show the promised thing immediately. No unrelated personality warm-up or "something crazy happened".
- Don't re-introduce the intro with more promises, housekeeping or "stay to the end". A branding monologue after a good opening is a second intro: cut it.
- Open with what people already believe, then twist it, without explaining the twist too early.
- Tie the intro's wording to the thumbnail image.
- Go from claim to on-screen proof fast (a real transaction, a real error, a real PR).
- **Open a loop:** the viewer knows the promise but not the process.
- If you mention a person or project, add a quick on-screen label so newcomers know who or what it is.
- Put catchphrases or mission lines inside the first content beat, never as a separate speech.

### Where AI fits
You choose the title, intro, framework and macro points. AI can then organise notes, gather examples and draft slides or support docs. For judging idea quality, a peer who knows both YouTube and your niche beats AI. Automate mechanical editing tasks if you like, but do the creative work by hand first.

## Structure and retention

### Timed beats

| Time | Beat | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00 to 0:15 | Intro | Confirms the package and buys about 45 more seconds of trust |
| ~1:00 | Re-engage | Viewers decide whether to keep watching. Finish a small first unit here |
| ~3:00 | Re-engage | Another keep-watching decision |
| ~6:00 | Re-engage | After this, sunk cost works in your favour |
| 6:00+ | Back half | Personality, stories, deep context |
| End | Fold into the last point | Then cut |

- Beats don't need exact timing: 0:50 or 1:20 is fine if it feels natural. The intro can stretch to 20 to 25 seconds; past 25 to 30 it starts to feel late.
- Follow the formula about 90% of the way, not rigidly. If two points genuinely need five minutes, cut what doesn't need to be there instead of crushing them.

### Re-engagements
A re-engagement is a **reason to keep watching**: a payoff, new stakes, the next step or a reminder of the bigger promise.

- Use **setup, knockdown, setup.** Each section resolves something and opens the next reason to watch.
- Good: "That was one major change, but it doesn't show the full picture." Bad: "watch to the end". Viewers recognise retention gaming.
- If a point runs past about 2 minutes, put a re-engagement inside it.
- Name the next section's value at each transition.
- In teaching videos, a re-engagement can be a question to the viewer, a hands-on detour, or a short aside after dry terminology.

### Front half, back half
- The front half earns distribution. The back half builds fans.
- **Front-load the payoffs.** Most viewers never reach the end, so they need several moments of learning throughout, not one at the end.
- If the first topic fits in 45 seconds, put it first. If it needs 2 minutes, lead with a shorter valuable point.
- Land the first one or two macro points within 3 minutes.
- Give a lot of value in the first six minutes, but leave the full system unresolved.
- Five-step example: step 1 done by about 0:45, step 2 by 3:00, step 3 by 6:00. Steps 4 and 5 can then take longer.
- Stay on what they clicked for. Background goes after 6:00, or gets one line.
- Order sections by dependency.
- Build big videos around one central moment where the topic allows.
- Many examples proving one point: a quick sped-up montage.
- If a subsection could be its own 20-minute video, summarise it and save the deep dive.

### Length
- **Go past 8 minutes,** where mid-roll ads unlock. Turning mid-rolls off while growing removes another reason to click away.
- A deep system video can run 15 to 20 minutes if every section earns its place. Past 20 needs a reason.

### Endings
- Once you signal the end, people start leaving, so long outros cause a steep drop.
- Fold the outro into the final point, say the closing line, add a short subscribe ask if you want one, pause briefly, cut.
- Make the ending **modular**: don't refer back to the section before it, so the editor can end after any section.

### When to break the formula
- Some huge videos ignore the beats entirely because the title and intro set up a moment the viewer waits for.
- For a deep dive aimed at experienced developers who already follow you, get the package and the first 15 seconds right, then let the material set the shape. These viewers skip to what they came for and watch to the end.
- Teaching, showing and entertaining well in one video every week is hard. On a weekly cadence, choose deliberately which of the three to do properly.

## Recording

- Keep the setup ready, so filming takes a few buttons, not a rebuild. Setup friction kills the will to film.
- Shoot more than one intro, and alternate takes of anything you're unsure about.
- Record the ending so it doesn't refer to the section before it.
- Talk to the editor in the footage ("don't use that one", "doing that again").
- Shoot optional back-half sections as standalone pieces.
- Redo stumbles cleanly. If the first half of a sentence isn't needed, say the whole line again without it.
- Shoot a 20-second version of any explanation that ran a minute.
- Check framing: sitting too close to the camera rules out some edits. A second angle raised to roughly 45 degrees can fix an awkward setup. Record the face camera as its own file, so it can be moved and resized.

## Editing

### Three passes
1. **Structure:** transitions, and whether each moment is full screen, desktop or face camera. No graphics yet.
2. **Full watch-through for flow:** the structural cut is only about 75% to 80% done. This pass finds missing context and ideas that don't connect.
3. **Polish and graphics last.** Edit as an editor, then watch as a viewer. The moment you reach for your phone, go back 30 seconds and fix what lost you.

### Cutting rules
- Hard-cut between completed thoughts, on the breath or reset, which gives viewers a beat to process dense material.
- **Treat the first six minutes as a gladiator pit:** the closer a line is to 0:00, the harder it must justify itself. Even a good joke early on may go.
- Match energy across cuts, not just words. Sometimes keep a few seconds that bridge the energy.
- Space, audio and silence carry the edit too. Knowing what to leave in matters.
- In tutorials, think before cutting the moment something fails: it's often half the lesson. Keep it when viewers need to follow the process, and agree it with the creator on the first version.
- Kill your darlings when a planned bit breaks the flow.
- Move slow explanations, anecdotes and tangents to the back half.
- Show screen proof early and often (transactions, errors, PRs, deploys), and run reinforcing examples quickly.
- Include optional sections in the first cut so pacing can be judged, then recommend what to drop.

### Graphics and motion
- Every graphic must be easy to understand and help communicate the idea. Don't add motion because it exists.
- A tiny zoom (around 10 to 15 pixels) suits softer or serious moments.
- Quick visuals and light music in the intro help, without making the rest feel slow by comparison.
- Small invisible polish counts: J-cuts, clean transitions, quiet sound effects under cuts.
- Use AI for graphics only as a tool. If it looks AI-generated, it's bad.
- Work from reference images and adjust them.

### Creator and editor roles
- The creator supplies the story and has the final yes or no. The editor owns execution and, over time, taste: fonts, graphic style, transitions, pacing and repeatable brand elements.
- Give concrete notes on what stays, what goes and where to speed up. The notes teach the editor your taste.
- Coach rather than replace. Build a mood board of clips, channels and styles, and record feedback meetings for reference.
- Ask for reshoots, especially of intros, rather than forcing a weak segment into the cut.

## Publish and distribute

- **Seed it:** before going public, share the unlisted video for a day with your most engaged community members. Their full watches place it in the right watch histories with high satisfaction.
- **Post to X and Discord the moment it goes live.** Early traction from your own people extends the first push.
- **News videos** (network upgrades, releases, exploits) go out the same day or the next morning, or the window closes.
- **Cadence:** one long-form video a week is the baseline. Be predictable: two one week and none the next makes it harder for YouTube to know when to push you. Scale (Shorts, streams, a second weekly video) only after the weekly routine works.
- **Other platforms:** treat Shorts, X and community posts as feeders for the main channel. X rewards quick thoughts more than polished takes, so be less precious there except around launches. Stream only if you enjoy it.

## Measure and repackage

### Read the retention graph
- Start with the first six minutes, where most people leave. Compare your line to YouTube Studio's typical range. Small plateaus are good.
- Expect a drop where you signal the end. A steep cliff means the outro is too long.
- AVD tells you the average. To know how many people reach a specific moment, read the graph at that moment.

### When to swap the title or thumbnail
- Bombed, underperforming or flatlined: swap. You can't make it worse.
- Still performing: leave it alone. Don't chase A/B tests endlessly.
- Evergreen videos that tapered off are good candidates even months later. For news videos, swap only on day one.
- Packaged for core fans: test broader packaging 48 to 72 hours later, once your core audience has seen it.
- Polish titles on older above-average videos: small gains on existing traffic add up.

### A/B tests
Testing starts to mean something only at substantial daily impressions (roughly thousands a day, as a rough feel rather than a significance threshold). Below that, trust your judgement.

## Build your content brain

- Watch 30 to 60 minutes a day (or 4 to 5 hours a week) of videos in your niche, passively but intentionally: understand why they made each choice.
- Use a separate Google account for research viewing, so your research feed and personal feed don't mix.
- Study outliers: when a channel's typical video gets X and one gets 10X, work out why.
- Watch inside the niche for audience expectations and outside it for taste. Translate what works into your voice.
- Study channels in your sub-genre rather than generic entertainment editing.
- Ask "who do I want to feel like?" rather than "who inspires me?". Inspiration drifts into mimicry.
- Review competitors weekly for intros, editing and packaging. Run a small writers' room with a few creators, bringing formed ideas.
- Turn work you're already doing into videos when it has enough structure.

## Web3 adaptations

### Stretch titles for Web3, AI, ZK and Web2

| Domain | Truth | Stretch (still deliverable) |
|---|---|---|
| Web3 | How To Deploy An ERC-20 Token With Foundry | Most Token Tutorials Leak Your Private Key |
| Web3 | Optimistic Vs ZK Rollups Explained | Why Your Rollup Withdrawal Takes A Week |
| AI | Few-Shot Prompting Experiments | One Example Made My Model Worse Than None |
| ZK | Writing A Noir Circuit | My Circuit Compiled And Proved The Wrong Thing |
| Web2 | Fixing PostgreSQL Too Many Connections | Raising max_connections Made It Worse |

Every stretch must still be paid off in the video, early.

### Thumbnail ideas
- **Enemies that every Web3 developer recognises:** a gas fee spike, a "transaction failed" toast, a reverted call in red, a drained-wallet alert, a stuck "pending" spinner.
- **Saviours:** a green "verified" badge, a passing test run, a confirmed transaction.
- Magnify the one line that matters: the revert reason, the gas number, the function signature.

### Web3 safety on screen
- **Never show a real private key, seed phrase or API key on screen,** even briefly. Blur or use a burner wallet with test funds only, and say so.
- Demo on a **testnet**, and label it.
- Show contract addresses only after verifying them on a block explorer, and state the network.
- **No price talk, no "this will pump", no investment calls.** Explain mechanisms. Disclose any token holdings or sponsorships.
- Label example contracts and circuits as unaudited.

### News and upgrades
Network upgrades, major releases and exploit post-mortems are time-sensitive. Ship within a day, keep the setup light, and link the primary source (the EIP, the release notes or the official post-mortem) in the description.

## Per-video checklist

### Before recording
- [ ] What did the viewer click for, and what do they get? That's what the video owes them.
- [ ] Title at the stretch level: deliverable, makes people think "huh?"
- [ ] Thumbnail concept drafted and passes the squint test
- [ ] Intro (15 to 20 seconds) written word for word and read aloud
- [ ] Framework and macro points written. Only one or two points? Make it a Short or a post
- [ ] First real point lands by about 3:00
- [ ] Searched the title idea on YouTube and watched the top results
- [ ] Every page, reference, example and testnet wallet open and ready
- [ ] Setup ready to film in a few clicks

### While recording
- [ ] Several intro takes, alternates for anything uncertain
- [ ] Modular ending that doesn't refer to the previous section
- [ ] Spoken notes to the editor
- [ ] Optional back-half sections shot standalone
- [ ] A 20-second version of any long explanation
- [ ] No real keys, seed phrases or API keys on screen

### In the edit
- [ ] Three passes: structure, flow, polish
- [ ] Re-engagements near 1:00, 3:00 and 6:00
- [ ] Every graphic communicates an idea
- [ ] Ends inside the last point, no separate outro
- [ ] Over 8 minutes

### Publishing
- [ ] Description: one or two plain sentences, then links (article, repo, newsletter)
- [ ] Chapters only if it's a reference-style tutorial
- [ ] Seeded to engaged community members while unlisted
- [ ] Posted to X and Discord at go-live

### Afterwards
- [ ] 2 to 3 days in: if packaged for core fans, test broader packaging
- [ ] First week: read retention over the opening six minutes and at the end
- [ ] Flatlined evergreen video: repackage

## Questions for your strategist and editor

- Where does the channel sit between broad, niche and passion project, and what does the existing catalogue imply?
- Which three videos would you repackage first, and what's the stretch title for each?
- Where do the 1, 3 and 6-minute beats fail on recent retention graphs?
- What does the editor need in raw footage: spoken notes, alternate intros, modular sections, a B-roll list?
- Is the camera framing too close? Do we want a second angle?
- Which channels go on the shared study list, and when do we compare notes?
- Is one video a week realistic? What's the minimum viable edit, and what recording friction can we remove?
- Who owns thumbnails, and do we need a portrait library shoot?
- Which format could repeat monthly without boxing the channel in?
- What does "flatlined" look like in numbers for this channel?

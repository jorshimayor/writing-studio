# Structure

Readers decide in seconds whether a piece has their answer, then most of them skim. Structure is what makes them stay: the right information in the right place, under headings that tell them where to look.

## Plan around the reader

Before outlining, answer:
- What does the reader already know? Familiarity with a word is not the same as understanding it.
- What do they want from this piece, and why now?
- What must they understand first for the main point to land?
- What do they need to decide or act?
- In what order should they meet each idea?

Every section should exist because it answers one of these.

## Rank the information

Every piece has three tiers:
1. **Primary:** the answer to the question in the title.
2. **Secondary:** context that makes the answer useful in practice.
3. **Tertiary:** depth for readers who want more.

Put them in that order (an inverted pyramid):
1. **Intro**: sets up the problem.
2. **Lead** (1 to 2 sections): the primary answer.
3. **Body** (1 to 3 sections): supporting context.
4. **Tail**: extra depth and related ideas.
5. **Next step**: where to go now (a CTA, a follow-up piece, a video).

Most pieces don't need a heading called "Conclusion". End on the next step.

## Write the intro last, and make it earn the click

The intro's job is to give the reader a reason to continue. It should:
- Set up the problem, situation or tension the topic lives in.
- Show why it matters to this reader.
- Imply what's coming, without listing it.

Two mistakes kill intros:
- **Giving the answer away.** If the first lines contain the full definition or verdict, the rest looks like filler and readers leave. Introduce *what will be discussed*, not *what it says*.
- **Announcing the structure.** "In this article, we will cover A, B and C" is a table of contents written as prose. Let the setup imply the roadmap instead.

A good intro ends by pointing straight at the first section's question.

## Lead with the core answer

The first major section delivers what the title promised. Context and nuance go in supporting paragraphs *after* the answer, not in a long lead-up to it. If a reader skims straight to the first heading, they should get value.

## Headings

Headings are for skimmers, and skimmers are most of your audience. A skimmer who finds a heading that matches their question stops and reads, often more carefully than someone who started at the top.

- Phrase H2s as the reader's real questions: "How Does Attention Pick Which Words Matter?" beats "Attention Mechanism".
- Make each heading specific enough to say what the section contains.
- Use H3s as direct signposts within a section, often just a name or a noun phrase.
- Never skip levels (H2 → H4).

## Sections

- **One purpose per section.** If you can't say what question a section answers, cut or merge it.
- **Order so each section prepares the next:** context → core idea → how it works → how it's used → implications. Adapt to the reader need.
- **Transitions:** end a section by raising the question the next heading answers, without writing "next we'll look at". If transitions are hard to write, the order is probably wrong.

## Paragraphs and sentences

- One idea per paragraph: introduce it, develop it, connect it to the section.
- Group related paragraphs. Don't bounce between subjects.
- 3 to 4 sentences is a good default. Clarity decides, not a word count.
- Mix sentence lengths. All short sentences sound curt. Long ones with many clauses force re-reading.
- The piece should make sense in one read. Second reads are for finding things again.

## Introduce ideas one at a time

Bring in each concept when the reader needs it, after showing why it will matter. Don't drop five new terms into one paragraph. Too little context confuses readers. Too much buries the point. A single explanatory clause is often enough: "LoRA, which fine-tunes a small set of added weights instead of the whole model, ..."

## Lists and tables

**Lists** for genuinely separate items: steps, requirements, options, pros and cons, examples. Not for continuous reasoning. If you're on your third list, check whether it's a habit. Keep list items grammatically parallel with the lead-in sentence.

**Tables** for comparing structured information across several options. Always:
1. Say what the table compares before it.
2. Interpret it after it: the two or three things the reader should notice.

A table dropped in without context pulls the reader out of the piece, and many won't come back.

## Length

As long as the reader's need requires and no longer. A short piece that fully answers the question beats a long one padded to hit a word count. Before keeping any passage, ask:
- Does the reader need this?
- Does it serve the piece's purpose?
- Does it help explain what comes before or after?
- Would the piece be worse without it?

If more than one answer is no, cut it.
